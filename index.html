Part 1 Australia and its People

Understanding Australia's community, population, Indigenous heritage, and how the nation developed into a stable, successful multicultural society.

Aboriginal & Torres Strait Islander peoples›

Australia's first inhabitants are the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, who have the oldest continuous cultures and traditions in the world.

The archaeological record indicates Aboriginal peoples arrived between 65,000 and 40,000 years ago. Aboriginal people are historically from mainland Australia and Tasmania; Torres Strait Islander people are from islands between the northern tip of Queensland and Papua New Guinea.

They have a deep connection with the land, expressed in their stories, art and dance. They make up approximately three per cent of the Australian population, and there are more than 100 distinct Indigenous languages.

European settlement & the gold rush›

European settlement began when the first 11 convict ships — the First Fleet — arrived from Great Britain on 26 January 1788. The first Governor of the colony of New South Wales was Captain Arthur Phillip.

British laws were harsh and jails were overcrowded, so convicts were transported to the new colony of New South Wales. Early free settlers came from Great Britain and Ireland — a heritage that strongly shaped Australia's culture and politics.

In 1851 a gold rush began in NSW and Victoria. Chinese people arriving then were the first large group of migrants not from Europe. In 10 years Australia's population more than doubled.

The nation of Australia & migration›

In 1901 the separate colonies united into a federation called the Commonwealth of Australia, establishing the national parliament, government and High Court under the new Constitution. The population was then about four million — a figure that did not include Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples until after the 1967 Referendum.

A wave of non-British migration came after World War II. Today migration and refugee programs bring people from all over the world. Australia's national language is English, an important part of national identity.

Traditions: Welcome & Acknowledgement of Country›

A Welcome to Country is performed by an Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander custodian of the local region, welcoming visitors to their traditional land. It can take the form of songs, dances, smoking ceremonies or speeches, and is usually the first item to open an event.

An Acknowledgement of Country recognises the gathering is on Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander land and pays respect to Traditional Custodians and Elders. Anyone can deliver it — usually the Chair or Master of Ceremonies.

Important days: Australia Day & Anzac Day›

Australia Day — 26 January. A public holiday in every state and territory, marking the anniversary of the arrival of the First Fleet in 1788. The biggest annual public holiday; the Prime Minister announces the Australian of the Year awards in Canberra.

Anzac Day — 25 April. Named after the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps, which landed at Gallipoli in Türkiye during WWI on 25 April 1915. A solemn day remembering all Australians who served and died in wars, conflicts and peacekeeping.

Part 2 Democratic Beliefs, Rights & Liberties

At the ceremony you pledge to share Australia's democratic beliefs and respect its rights and liberties. Here they all are.

Our four democratic beliefs›
  • Parliamentary democracy — government power comes from the people, because citizens vote for representatives who must answer to them through elections.
  • The Rule of Law — all Australians are equal under the law; no person, group or religious rule is above the law, including government, community and religious leaders, business people and police.
  • Living peacefully — change should occur through discussion, peaceful persuasion and the democratic process. We reject violence.
  • Respect for all individuals regardless of race, origin, gender, sexual orientation, marital status, age, disability, heritage, culture, politics, wealth or religion.
Our freedoms›
  • Freedom of speech & expression — say, write and discuss your ideas; criticise government and protest peacefully — while obeying the law.
  • Freedom of association — form and join (or not join) any legal group: political party, trade union, religious, cultural or social. Nobody can be forced.
  • Freedom of religion — Australia has no official national religion; the government is secular. Where law and religious practice conflict, Australian law prevails.
Our equalities — and a 'fair go'›

Gender equality: men and women have equal rights; it is against the law to discriminate based on gender.

Equality of opportunity ('a fair go'): what you achieve should come from your talents, work and effort — not wealth or background. There are no formal class distinctions in Australian society.

Responsibilities & privileges of citizenship›

Responsibilities — what you give Australia:

  • Obey the laws of Australia
  • Vote in federal, state/territory elections and referenda
  • Defend Australia should the need arise
  • Serve on a jury if called

Privileges — what Australia gives you:

  • Vote in elections and referenda
  • Apply for children born overseas to be citizens by descent
  • Apply for jobs in the Australian Public Service / Defence Force
  • Seek election to parliament  ·  Apply for an Australian passport & re-enter freely
  • Ask for consular assistance overseas

Voting is compulsory in federal and state/territory elections for citizens aged 18+.

Part 3 Government and the Law

How Australia's parliamentary democracy works — voting, the Constitution, the separation of powers, and how laws are made and enforced.

How I have my say — voting›

Citizens aged 18+ must enrol to vote in federal, state/territory elections and referenda. Voting is compulsory and by secret ballot.

The Australian Electoral Commission (AEC) runs federal elections and referendums and is independent of government — parties and politicians cannot influence it. Failing to vote without a good reason can mean a fine. Citizens can also contact their elected representative to raise concerns.

Federation & the Australian Constitution›

Before 1901, Australia was six separate self-governing British colonies, each with its own constitution and laws. On 1 January 1901 they united into the Commonwealth of Australia.

The Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act 1900 is the legal document setting the basic rules of government. It established the Parliament (House of Representatives + Senate) and the High Court. It can only be changed by a referendum requiring a 'double majority' — a majority of voters nationally and a majority of voters in a majority of states.

The three powers (separation of powers)›
  • Legislative power — Parliament makes and changes laws.
  • Executive power — the Prime Minister, ministers and Governor-General put laws into practice.
  • Judicial power — judges interpret and apply the law; courts are independent of parliament and government.

Dividing power this way stops one person or group from holding all the power.

Head of State & constitutional monarchy›

Australia's Head of State is the King of Australia, His Majesty King Charles III. The King appoints the Governor-General as his representative (on the PM's advice). Australia is a constitutional monarchy: the King is Head of State but must act in accordance with the Constitution. The Prime Minister is the leader of the Australian Government.

The Governor-General gives Royal Assent to Bills, performs ceremonial duties, starts federal elections, is Commander-in-Chief of the Defence Force, and holds special 'reserve powers'.

Parliament: the two Houses›

House of Representatives (Lower / People's House): over 150 members; the number per state/territory depends on population. MPs represent electorates.

Senate (Upper / House of Review / States' House): 76 senators — 12 from each state and 2 each from the ACT and NT. All states are equally represented regardless of size.

Three levels of government›
  • Federal: taxation, defence, immigration & citizenship, foreign affairs, social security, postal & communications, trade, airports.
  • State / Territory: hospitals, schools, roads & railways, police & ambulance, public transport, forestry.
  • Local councils: local roads & footpaths, rubbish collection, parks, building permits, libraries, animal control.

States have rights recognised by the Constitution; territory laws can be altered or revoked by the Australian Government.

Political parties & forming government›

Main parties: Liberal Party of Australia, Australian Labor Party, the Nationals, the Australian Greens. Members with no party are independents.

After an election, government is formed by the party or coalition with the majority in the House of Representatives; its leader becomes Prime Minister. The second-largest forms the Opposition. The most senior ministers form the Cabinet — the key decision-making body.

Enforcing the law: courts, juries & police›

Courts are independent and decide on the evidence before them. Judges & magistrates are the highest authority in court; government cannot remove them for their decisions.

Juries are ordinary citizens randomly chosen; people are innocent until proven guilty and everyone has the right to a lawyer. The police protect life and property, are independent of government, and the national force is the Australian Federal Police (AFP). It is a serious crime to bribe police.

Part 4 Australian Values

Values based on freedom, respect, fairness and equality of opportunity — central to keeping Australia secure, prosperous and peaceful.

Commitment to the Rule of Law›

All Australians are protected by, and equal under, the law. No person or group is above the law. You should obey the law even if no one is watching — regardless of your background or culture.

Parliamentary democracy›

Laws are determined by parliaments elected by the people. Government power comes from the people through their vote. Voting is compulsory, showing the importance of participating.

Freedom of speech, association & religion›

Express ideas freely within the law; meet for social or political discussion; media share the same freedom. Peaceful protest is essential to democracy.

It is never acceptable to promote violence against a person or group. Religious laws have no legal status; practices such as polygamy and forced marriage are against the law.

Equality of all people under the law›

Equal rights regardless of gender, sexual orientation, age, disability, religion, race or origin. Two people can marry each other, including two men or two women. Men and women have equal access to education, employment, voting and the Defence/police forces. Divorce is acceptable.

Equality of opportunity — a 'fair go'›

Everyone deserves a fair go, free of class distinction. Success should come from hard work and talent; jobs go to the person with the best skills and experience — not gender, wealth or ethnicity.

Mutual respect & tolerance for others›

Respect the freedom, dignity, opinions and choices of others. All violence — verbal or physical — is illegal. The age of sexual consent is 16 or 17 depending on the state/territory. Racism has no place in Australia, including online or at sporting events.

Our community: contribution, mateship & loyalty›

Everyone is expected to participate and support themselves and their families where able. Australians value 'mateship' — helping each other in times of need — and a strong tradition of volunteering.

English is valued as the unifying national language. We help keep society safe (report serious crime; report child abuse to police; cyber abuse is not accepted). In the pledge, citizens promise loyalty to Australia; dual citizenship is allowed, but Australian law must always be followed.

★ The Australian Citizenship Pledge ★

"From this time forward,* under God,
I pledge my loyalty to Australia and its people,
whose democratic beliefs I share,
whose rights and liberties I respect, and
whose laws I will uphold and obey."

*A person may choose whether or not to use the words 'under God'.
